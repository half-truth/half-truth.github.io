> **What is the cash value of a secret, and how could one be priced fairly?**  
>
> This is a tricky problem, particularly for secrets about people, because by definition a secret is something that few individuals will initially understand or appreciate. I propose the following basic principle: the fair value of a secret is the sum of all future net earnings this secret will generate for its owner, discounted for time and risk.
>
> The major obstacles towards converging on a fair price for a secret are two-fold. First, it's impossible to practically forecast the sum of all future earnings a secret is capable of generating, and even the most well thought out confidence interval will be severely wide. Second, discounting for risk is really tricky—it's difficult to evaluate a business model based on something that by definition very few people understand. So in our three-variable equation (future net earnings, time discount, risk discount) at least two are very difficult to nail down.

> **Is there a market for secrets, and what's the opportunity for brokerage?**
>
> As it turns out, there is: Venture Capital. The market for secrets today is Sand Hill Road, and its currency is the term sheet. While VCs everywhere preach about all the value they bring to companies, what they really do is act as secret brokerage firms. When a VC invests in a startup, they are betting that they've invested at a lower price than the fair value of this secret. Whenever a VC gets an exit, they have successfully brokered a secret between its discoverers and its new owners, and their return represents a broker's fee.
>
> Be aware there's a catch: the market is really inefficient. Most VC firms fail to beat the market these days, and VCs are getting a stupidly good deal—if their investments pay off, they make twenty; if they lose everything, they still make two. The old school VC model is starting to get attacked from all sides, with early stage financing (AngelList), network & prestige (Y Combinator) getting unbundled and optimized.

> **Can such a market ever theoretically be made efficient?**
>
> I'm not sure it can: a genuinely valuable secret's true worth is almost inversely proportional to its publicly perceived value, so true competition cannot really occur. Secrets have the exact opposite problem of the auction-winner's curse: a genuine secret marketplace is one **where consensus value and true value are perfectly uncoupled**. If this is true, then the secret trade is therefore **the most theoretically profitable industry that can exist!**
>
