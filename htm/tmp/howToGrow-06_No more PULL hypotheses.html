I have often talked about a thing called a PULL hypothesis, which states:

“I believe this person has PULL. They are trying to do X right now, but can’t do it with Y because Z - and therefore would be weird not to buy a product that un-blocks them so they can get X done.”

The PULL hypothesis was part of the minimalist way to go from 0-1: Craft a PULL hypothesis, then immediately test it by trying to sell to 5 potential customers. See what happens, iterate, repeat.

A bunch of startups have used this method to take off. But I’ve noticed that some founders misinterpret the word “hypothesis”, and their misinterpretation causes them to get stuck.

“Hypothesis” comes with baggage - it feels like the kind of thing that originates in a scientist’s brain. Which leads a lot of founders to sit around and guess as a way to create their PULL hypothesis. (This happens despite the “Real Person Rule” in my book, which states that a PULL hypothesis must be based on a real person with PULL!)

Guessing is basically guaranteed to create a PULL hypothesis that is wrong. There are infinite PULL hypotheses you can create, but there are a comparatively tiny number of real PULL frameworks that fit even one real person in the real world right now. Worse, the PULL hypotheses that you can plausibly craft could very well have zero overlap with any real PULL:

It doesn’t matter if a hypothesis makes sense in your head. The odds are probably a billion-to-one that your hypothesis is wrong when it originates from your brain.1

Now, having a wrong PULL hypothesis wouldn’t be a big deal… if we didn’t cling so tightly to our hypothesis.

But there’s something about the idea of a hypothesis that causes us to create an imaginary world in our minds in which our hypothesis is true. We get invested in that imaginary world, and it prevents us from seeing the real world.

As a result:

  1. We create a PULL hypothesis that has roughly zero chance of being right, based on abstract logic about market dynamics or trends or social media posts.

  2. We schedule calls to test this hypothesis.

  3. There isn’t PULL, but we want there to be PULL, so we see PULL where it isn’t (and don’t throw away our hypothesis)

Or worse:

  • We craft a bunch of different plausible hypotheses

  • We try to big-brain our way to the “right” one

  • The only progress we make is giving ourselves a concussion

Of course, this doesn’t happen every time. But it happens. So the word “hypothesis” must go. Hypotheses non fingo.

PULL Hypothesis → PULL Anecdote

How do you build without a hypothesis?

You start with a PULL anecdote: One person with real PULL right now.

Anecdotes, of course, may not replicate. But it is infinitely more likely for an anecdote to replicate than it is for any random PULL hypothesis to find a single anecdote.

In the 0-1 stage, therefore, your job is to operate without a PULL hypothesis and just find a PULL anecdote.

  • It could be you - a thing you personally experienced that you would pay real money to solve, but you can’t find good options.

  • Or it could be someone else - someone who is willing to pay you to do something for them.

Once you find a PULL anecdote, you then can then see if that anecdote replicates, and decide whether you can - or even want to - conjure supply to serve it.

After the 0-1 stage, your job is to avoid hypothesizing and look to your anecdotes.

Unless you have an anecdote, you have nothing. There is no hypothesis.

PS: This sets you free

I did a workshop this week with a company that was overwhelmed trying to select among a million PULL hypotheses - all of which sounded plausible and compelling. When we ruled out the idea of the hypothesis and instead looked at real PULL anecdotes, their path became blindingly obvious. I shudder to think what would have come out of the session if we hadn’t eliminated the hypothesis.

Corollaries:

  • Eliminating the hypothesis an application of the “No Speculation” rule.

  • I have previously written about how zero-to-one is really infinity-to-one; turns out, the secret to navigating this stage is avoiding the infinite.

  • This makes the PULL method/universe much simpler and more elegant. I love it.

  • I thought I was quite clever with this, and then I learned that Francis Bacon understood this back in the 1600s - specifically in Novum Organum, with “Anticipations of Nature” and “Idols of the Theatre”. I’m just 400 years behind.
